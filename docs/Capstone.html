Part 1. Introduction and Research Questions

1.1 Background

Two people can walk away from the same conversation with different impressions of how it went. Perceived interaction quality refers here to a person’s subjective evaluation of a particular interaction, including how engaging, smooth, natural, comfortable, and positive it felt. This project examines whether nine questionnaire items can be represented by a common perceived interaction quality factor and whether conversation partners’ latent evaluations are associated.

1.2 Research Questions

RQ1. How well does a one-factor measurement model account for responses to the perceived interaction quality items?

RQ2. To what extent are conversation partners’ latent evaluations of interaction quality associated?

RQ3. Are the findings sensitive to allowing a residual covariance between QI8 and QI9 or removing these two items?

1.3 Expectations and Analytic Approach

Because both partners experienced the same conversation, their evaluations may be positively associated. Three measurement specifications are examined: 1. An initial nine-item one-factor model. 2. A nine-item model allowing a residual covariance between QI8 and QI9. 3. A seven-item model excluding QI8 and QI9.

The seven-item model is an exploratory sensitivity analysis motivated by discussion of item content. QI8 and QI9 explicitly assess comfort and positive feelings. However, the distinction between these items and QI1–QI7 is not absolute: QI3 also assesses enjoyment. Model selection is not based on obtaining the largest partner correlation.

1.4 Dataset

The dataset includes 218 participants across 46 sessions, with ratings from both partners for 412 conversations. Within each session, participants spoke with multiple partners in a round-robin design. Each conversation contributed two sets of ratings, and each participant could contribute ratings for several conversations.

Note. The data were provided by Dr. Erin Heerey’s lab and are restricted to course use. The analysis code documents how the data were prepared and analyzed.

fmt_p <- function(x) {
  if (is.na(x)) {
    return("p unavailable")
  }

  if (x < .001) {
    return("p < .001")
  }

  paste0("p = ", sprintf("%.3f", x))
}

Part 3. Preliminary One-Factor CFA

3.1 Purpose and Estimation

The preliminary models examine the item measurement structure before partners’ responses are modeled jointly. This is a diagnostic step rather than a required prerequisite for every dyadic CFA.

These models pool individual rating records and do not account for repeated participation or reciprocal ratings. Their fit statistics and standard errors are therefore interpreted as preliminary.

For consistency with the original analyses, all models use maximum likelihood estimation, treat the seven-category items as continuous (1 = Strongly disagree , 7 = Strongly agree), and use full-information maximum likelihood for missing item responses. Factor variances are fixed to one using std.lv = TRUE.

Part 7. Results, Interpretation, and Limitations

7.1 Selection of the Final Model

D2, the nine-item dyadic model with separate QI8–QI9 residual covariances for each partner, was selected as the primary exploratory model for four reasons. First, D2 fit better than D1, which uses the same nine items without the residual covariances. CFI and TLI increased, RMSEA and SRMR decreased, and the chi-square difference was 355.29 (df = 132, p < .001). Second, D2 matched or modestly outperformed the seven-item model. CFI and TLI were essentially the same, whereas RMSEA and SRMR were lower for D2. Because the seven- and nine-item models contain different indicators, this comparison is descriptive. Third, QI8 and QI9 were substantial indicators of the common factor. Removing them reduced the content coverage of the scale without producing a clearly better measurement model. Fourth, the partner latent correlation was nearly unchanged across D1, D2, and D3, so the decision does not depend on obtaining a larger correlation. D2 is described as acceptable rather than excellent: CFI was just below .95, RMSEA was in the acceptable range, and some local misfit remained. Because the model was modified after examining the data and does not account for dependence across conversations, it is treated as an exploratory final model rather than a confirmed measurement model.

7.2 Preliminary Measurement Results

The initial nine-item one-factor model yielded χ²(27) = 275.15, p < .001, CFI = 0.937, TLI = 0.916, RMSEA = 0.106, 90% CI [0.094, 0.117], SRMR = 0.037. Allowing a residual covariance between QI8 and QI9 yielded χ²(26) = 217.87, p < .001, CFI = 0.951, TLI = 0.932, RMSEA = 0.095, 90% CI [0.083, 0.106], SRMR = 0.035. The seven-item sensitivity model yielded χ²(14) = 139.50, p < .001, CFI = 0.954, TLI = 0.931, RMSEA = 0.104, 90% CI [0.089, 0.120], SRMR = 0.037. In the current results, allowing the QI8–QI9 residual covariance improved the descriptive fit of the nine-item model. Removing QI8 and QI9 did not produce a consistent improvement across fit indices. Because the seven- and nine-item models contain different observed indicators, their fit indices are interpreted descriptively rather than as a conventional nested-model comparison. Improved fit does not, by itself, establish that the revised model adequately captures every aspect of the item relationships.

7.3 Dyadic Measurement Results

The initial nine-item dyadic model yielded χ²(134) = 417.82, p < .001, CFI = 0.929, TLI = 0.919, RMSEA = 0.072, 90% CI [0.064, 0.080], SRMR = 0.043. The revised nine-item dyadic model, including separate QI8–QI9 residual covariances within each partner, yielded χ²(132) = 355.29, p < .001, CFI = 0.944, TLI = 0.935, RMSEA = 0.064, 90% CI [0.056, 0.072], SRMR = 0.042. The seven-item dyadic model yielded χ²(76) = 229.09, p < .001, CFI = 0.945, TLI = 0.934, RMSEA = 0.070, 90% CI [0.060, 0.080], SRMR = 0.045. These dyadic fit results must be evaluated separately from the preliminary individual-record CFA results.

7.4 Partner Latent Association

The estimated partner latent correlation was 0.354 in the initial nine-item dyadic model, 0.354 in the revised nine-item model, and 0.345 in the seven-item model. The seven-item estimate differed from the initial nine-item estimate by -0.009. In the current results, these similar point estimates suggest that the positive association between partners’ latent evaluations was not driven solely by QI8 and QI9. This finding concerns association rather than exact agreement. A positive correlation indicates that partners’ evaluations tend to vary together across conversations; it does not imply identical ratings or equal average levels. The sensitivity analysis does not establish statistical equivalence between the correlations, and changes in the point estimates are not treated as evidence of statistically significant differences.

7.5 Interpretation of QI8 and QI9

QI8 and QI9 may both reflect general interaction quality while also sharing additional content related to comfort and positive feelings. Allowing a residual covariance acknowledges shared variation not captured by the general factor. It does not establish that the additional variation represents a distinct positive-affect factor or identify its cause. The seven-item analysis provides an alternative operationalization with narrower item coverage. Its results are useful for evaluating sensitivity to item inclusion, not for choosing whichever specification produces the largest partner correlation.

7.6 Limitations

First, participants contributed ratings to multiple conversations within sessions. The current dyadic models represent dependence between the two partners in a conversation through their factor covariance, but they do not account for dependence across conversations arising from repeated participants or shared sessions. Fit statistics, standard errors, and population-level inference therefore remain preliminary. Second, A and B are assigned by participant ID rather than by theoretically meaningful roles. Separate measurement parameters are estimated for each position. These models do not establish exchangeability or measurement equivalence across partners. Partner equality constraints and the implications of the labeling scheme require further consideration. Third, the seven-category item responses are treated as continuous under maximum likelihood estimation. Analyses using an ordinal-data estimator could be considered as an additional sensitivity analysis, with appropriate attention to differences in missing-data handling and model estimation. Fourth, the QI8–QI9 residual covariance and seven-item specification were examined after inspecting the data and discussing item content. These analyses are exploratory and would benefit from evaluation in an independent sample. Finally, the latent partner association is conditional on the specified measurement model. Remaining local misfit, omitted cross-partner item associations, or other measurement assumptions may affect its interpretation.

# Part 1. Introduction and Research Questions

## 1.1 Background
Two people can walk away from the same conversation with different impressions of how it went. Perceived interaction quality refers here to a person's subjective evaluation of a particular interaction, including how engaging, smooth, natural, comfortable, and positive it felt.
This project examines whether nine questionnaire items can be represented by a common perceived interaction quality factor and whether conversation partners' latent evaluations are associated.

## 1.2 Research Questions
**RQ1.** How well does a one-factor measurement model account for responses to the perceived interaction quality items?

**RQ2.** To what extent are conversation partners' latent evaluations of interaction quality associated?

**RQ3.** Are the findings sensitive to allowing a residual covariance between QI8 and QI9 or removing these two items?

## 1.3 Expectations and Analytic Approach
Because both partners experienced the same conversation, their evaluations may be positively associated.
Three measurement specifications are examined:
**1.** An initial nine-item one-factor model.
**2.** A nine-item model allowing a residual covariance between QI8 and QI9.
**3.** A seven-item model excluding QI8 and QI9.

The seven-item model is an exploratory sensitivity analysis motivated by discussion of item content. QI8 and QI9 explicitly assess comfort and positive feelings. However, the distinction between these items and QI1–QI7 is not absolute: QI3 also assesses enjoyment.
Model selection is not based on obtaining the largest partner correlation.

## 1.4 Dataset
The dataset includes 218 participants across 46 sessions, with ratings from both partners for 412 conversations. Within each session, participants spoke with multiple partners in a round-robin design. Each conversation contributed two sets of ratings, and each participant could contribute ratings for several conversations.

**Note.** The data were provided by Dr. Erin Heerey's lab and are restricted to course use. The analysis code documents how the data were prepared and analyzed.

# Part 7. Results, Interpretation, and Limitations
## 7.1 Selection of the Final Model
D2, the nine-item dyadic model with separate QI8–QI9 residual covariances for each partner, was selected as the primary exploratory model for four reasons.
First, D2 fit better than D1, which uses the same nine items without the residual covariances. CFI and TLI increased, RMSEA and SRMR decreased, and the chi-square difference was 355.29 (df = 132, p < .001).
Second, D2 matched or modestly outperformed the seven-item model. CFI and TLI were essentially the same, whereas RMSEA and SRMR were lower for D2. Because the seven- and nine-item models contain different indicators, this comparison is descriptive.
Third, QI8 and QI9 were substantial indicators of the common factor. Removing them reduced the content coverage of the scale without producing a clearly better measurement model.
Fourth, the partner latent correlation was nearly unchanged across D1, D2, and D3, so the decision does not depend on obtaining a larger correlation.
D2 is described as acceptable rather than excellent: CFI was just below .95, RMSEA was in the acceptable range, and some local misfit remained. Because the model was modified after examining the data and does not account for dependence across conversations, it is treated as an exploratory final model rather than a confirmed measurement model.

## 7.2 Preliminary Measurement Results
The initial nine-item one-factor model yielded `r fit_text(qi_fit1)`. Allowing a residual covariance between QI8 and QI9 yielded `r fit_text(qi_fit2)`. The seven-item sensitivity model yielded `r fit_text(qi_fit3)`. In the current results, allowing the QI8–QI9 residual covariance improved the descriptive fit of the nine-item model. ## 7.5 Interpretation of QI8 and QI9
QI8 and QI9 may both reflect general interaction quality while also sharing additional content related to comfort and positive feelings. Allowing a residual covariance acknowledges shared variation not captured by the general factor. It does not establish that the additional variation represents a distinct positive-affect factor or identify its cause. The seven-item analysis provides an alternative operationalization with narrower item coverage. Its results are useful for evaluating sensitivity to item inclusion, not for choosing whichever specification produces the largest partner correlation.

## 7.6 Limitations
First, participants contributed ratings to multiple conversations within sessions. The current dyadic models represent dependence between the two partners in a conversation through their factor covariance, but they do not account for dependence across conversations arising from repeated participants or shared sessions. Fit statistics, standard errors, and population-level inference therefore remain preliminary.
Second, A and B are assigned by participant ID rather than by theoretically meaningful roles. Separate measurement parameters are estimated for each position. These models do not establish exchangeability or measurement equivalence across partners. Partner equality constraints and the implications of the labeling scheme require further consideration.
Third, the seven-category item responses are treated as continuous under maximum likelihood estimation. Analyses using an ordinal-data estimator could be considered as an additional sensitivity analysis, with appropriate attention to differences in missing-data handling and model estimation.
Fourth, the QI8–QI9 residual covariance and seven-item specification were examined after inspecting the data and discussing item content. These analyses are exploratory and would benefit from evaluation in an independent sample.
Finally, the latent partner association is conditional on the specified measurement model. Remaining local misfit, omitted cross-partner item associations, or other measurement assumptions may affect its interpretation.
